## Task 1 - Stakeholder, GQM, and Code Smell Selection

**Selected stakeholder and exact fictional concern from handout:** Scenario B - Maya, Technical-Debt Manager:
"We have limited development time, and some implementation choices could make future changes more expensive. We cannot improve everything. Please investigate suspected technical debt, compare its likely impact and the effort to address it, and justify which small improvements are worth making now."

**GQM goal:** Analyze **the handwritten Python source of the `ultralytics/` package** for the purpose of **identifying and prioritizing technical debt worth repaying now**, with respect to **maintainability, i.e. the cost of future change caused by duplication and complexity**, from the viewpoint of **Maya, the technical-debt manager**, in the context of **an individual MSc lab with a few hours of effort on a fork of a fast-evolving open-source computer-vision library (upstream > 5,700 commits)**.

| Initial question | Question wording | Relevant metric(s) / observation(s) | Why useful? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Q1** | Where in `ultralytics/` are duplication and complexity symptoms concentrated? | jscpd clones and duplicated lines (per file, total %); Ruff C901 cyclomatic complexity per function, PLR0912 branches, PLR0915 statements, PLR0913 parameters; radon CC/MI for the file finally refactored | Locates *candidate* debt. On its own it says nothing about cost - a complex function nobody changes charges no interest |
| **Q2** | Which of those symptoms sit in code that is actually changing, so the debt is already charging interest, and what would repayment cost? | Commits touching the file in the last 12 months (`git log --since`); number of recent commits that had to apply the **same edit to several clone instances**; number of dependent modules; estimated lines to change and whether a cheap, exact regression check is possible | Turns "warning count" into Maya's trade-off: likely future cost (interest) vs repayment effort and behavior risk |

**Code-smell types chosen from the lecture** (slide 32, "Major smells in object oriented programming"; individual: 4, from three of its five groups):

| Smell type | Lecture slide(s) | Why relevant to our GQM? | How we plan to investigate it (tool/rule/metric/inspection) |
|---|---|---|---|

| 3. Long Parameter List | 32 - *Bloaters* | Many parameters can make call sites fragile and APIs hard to evolve | **Automatic rule**: Ruff PLR0913 (> 5 args) + inspection of whether parameters are optional keyword options |
| 4. Shotgun Surgery | 32 - *Change preventers* | One logical change spread over many places is the clearest observable "interest" payment (Q2) | **Manual inspection with GitHub-visible evidence**: no linter rule exists; upstream commit history showing the same edit repeated in several places, linked to commits |

**One warning we challenged:** Ruff PLR0913 reports **230** "too many arguments" findings, e.g. [`plot_images`](https://github.com/yar01110/ultralytics/blob/2ac6f4f07acb447f294593236fbff8f83ab51678/ultralytics/utils/plotting.py#L881-L895) with 13 parameters. On inspection, 12 of the 13 are optional keyword options with defaults (`max_size`, `conf_thres`, `show_conf`, ...) documented in the docstring; callers pass only what they need. PLR0913 counts defaulted options exactly like required positional arguments, so it measures "how configurable is this function", not "how fragile are its call sites". We treat most of these as **likely false alarms for the Long Parameter List smell** and did not prioritise them. Conversely, the duplication tool is the **only** tool that sees C1: radon rates `kalman_filter.py` grade **A** (average CC 1.57, MI 49.8), so a complexity-only investigation would have missed it entirely.

**Answers to the original GQM questions:**

- **Q1 (where are the symptoms concentrated?):** Complexity is concentrated in a few orchestration functions - `Exporter.__call__` (CC 88), `_convert_ndjson_to_yolo` (67), `BaseTrainer._do_train` (42) - with 93 C901 findings package-wide. Duplication is low overall (0.82 %) but clustered in SAM model code, the tracker utilities (`kalman_filter.py`, `gmc.py`) and the per-task validators. The two symptom types point at **different** places: the most duplicated file (Kalman) is graded A for complexity, so neither metric alone locates the debt.
- **Q2 (which debt is charging interest and what does repayment cost?):** The Kalman duplication is the clearest case of observed interest - 3 of its last 7 code-changing commits made the same edit twice - and it had the lowest repayment cost (1 file, -166 lines, exact automatic verification). `Exporter.__call__` changes far more often (227 commits / 12 months), so its interest is probably higher, but we have no evidence that its size caused extra work per change, and repayment would be large and hard to verify; it is deferred, not dismissed.

**Conclusion and limits:** We removed one verified source of repeated work: the Kalman filter algebra now exists once, and the only difference between the XYAH and XYWH filters is stated in one small hook. Duplication fell from 378 to 292 lines package-wide and from 4 clones to 0 in the refactored file, with bit-identical outputs on the golden master. **Not improved / worsened:** Ruff's 443 findings are unchanged (C1 was never a Ruff finding); radon's maintainability index **dropped** from 49.84 to 40.61 because MI rewards comment/docstring lines and the removed copies carried docstrings - this shows MI is a proxy, not maintainability itself; average CC rose slightly (1.57 -> 1.73) because fewer trivial methods remain to average over. **What cannot be claimed:** that future maintenance cost actually falls (interest is inferred from 3 past commits, not measured going forward); that behavior is preserved beyond the tested inputs; that the hook design is what upstream maintainers would accept. Readers of `KalmanFilterXYWH` must now look at the base class to see the full algorithm - a small cost in local readability traded for one place to change.
